Every Compound. Every Reason.

By Shady Biskaly, R.Ph., BScPharm · Pharmacist and founder

Twelve ingredients. A proprietary blend. A label that reads like the cast of a Marvel movie. Impressive, right up until you ask how much of anything is actually in the bottle.

An ingredient list is easy to make impressive. A formula has to do harder work. Which extract? What dose? Studied in whom? And did the researchers measure anything relevant to the moment you're buying it for?

Those are pharmacist questions. Inconvenient for a label. Useful for the person paying for it.

Four compounds. A reason for each. Our standard for the finished label is straightforward: print the amounts and make the reasoning easy to inspect. Final extracts and doses are still being worked out.

L-Theanine: For the Committee Meeting in Your Head.

Walk into a room that matters and the running commentary starts. Am I talking too fast? Why did I say that? They're checking their phone. Congratulations. You're delivering a presentation and hosting a hostile podcast about your own performance.

L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea. In a small, placebo-controlled study of twelve participants doing a mental-arithmetic stress task, it reduced heart-rate and salivary immunoglobulin A responses relative to placebo. Twelve people won't settle the subject. But an actual stress task tells me more than a stock photo of someone meditating on a cliff. Read the L-theanine study.

Sceletium: The One They Tested on People About to Speak.

Researchers gave people a dose and then made them give a speech. Not a memory quiz. Not a puzzle. The thing people would pay good money to feel more composed doing. That got my attention.

A 2020 paper tested a single dose of a standardized Sceletium extract in two small studies. The public-speaking study found lower subjective anxiety at the prestress measurement and a treatment-by-time interaction in heart rate. The multitasking study found no treatment effect. Both results belong in the conversation. Read the Sceletium paper.

The small print belongs on the page. Small studies. Different results across tasks. Interesting enough to investigate; nowhere near a permission slip to promise the moon.

Saffron: Your Interview Is This Afternoon.

A supplement that needs several weeks to show an effect may have a perfectly respectable purpose. Helping you with the interview at two o'clock is a different job. The calendar doesn't care how attractive the ingredient sounds.

Our research list includes a 2023 trial of a specific saffron extract and its main volatile compound during an acute stress challenge. The extract, study design and timing all matter when deciding what that research can tell us about a future shot. Read the saffron study.

Caffeine: This Is Not a Pre-Workout Competition.

You have to introduce yourself. You do not have to deadlift the conference table. The goal is to stay engaged, not win an arms race in caffeine milligrams.

Caffeine belongs in the formulation discussion alongside individual sensitivity. A controlled study examined anxiety responses at different doses and their relationship to genetic differences. It does not give any dose a universal “no jitters” guarantee. Read the caffeine study.

That's why the amount matters as much as the ingredient. A bigger number looks exciting on a label. Your nervous system gets the final vote.

Check My Work. Then Watch the Next Step.

I've tried the ingredients individually myself. That experience helped shape Social Phix. These papers concern individual ingredients and specific study preparations; they do not test the finished Social Phix combination.

The work now is formulation, manufacturing and testing the shot. We'll publish final amounts and directions when they're ready. No fairy dust. No pretending the work is finished before it is.
